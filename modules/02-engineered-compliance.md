        ## 🧠 1. Theoretical Context: EdTech as Behavioral Engineering and Radical Monopoly

        This week, we bridge the gap between commercial attention-capture mechanics and the infrastructure of higher education[cite: 5]. While B.J. Fogg outlines the modern formula for corporate behavior manipulation ($B=MAP$), Audrey Watters exposes how modern Educational Technology (EdTech) frameworks are rooted directly in the behaviorist mechanics of B.F. Skinner[cite: 5].

        Ivan Illich expands this critique by showing how these automated systems mutate into a **Radical Monopoly**. A radical monopoly goes beyond typical commercial dominance; it occurs when an industrial process exercises an exclusive control over the satisfaction of a poignant need, eliminating human choice. When centralized learning dashboards, algorithmic tracking portals, and remote proctoring networks become the *only* entry point for education, they create a radical monopoly that forces students to surrender their behavioral data simply to participate in society.

        These platforms are marketed using the language of efficiency and personalization[cite: 5]. However, as Watters notes, their codebases are engineered to monitor student usage data, track user cadence, and nudge students into predictable execution channels[cite: 5]. This mirrors the Vatican’s warning in *Antiqua et nova* regarding **"Rapidification"** (§67)—the systematic acceleration of tasks that deskills the human operator and forces them to adapt to the frantic pace of an opaque machine pipeline[cite: 5].
